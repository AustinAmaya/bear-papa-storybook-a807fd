The Missing Way Home

Bear, Papa, and Sentinel follow sparkling portal dust to the Blue Lake and carefully rebuild the missing route home.

Preamble

The square sun was setting behind Bear and Papa’s Diamond House when Bear pulled the return lever.

Nothing happened.

The obsidian doorway stayed gray. No purple swirls. No view of Papa Bear Home.

Papa checked the redstone line, then knelt beside Bear. “We are safe here. But right now, we truly do not know how to get home.”

Bear breathed in slowly. Then out.

Sentinel stood guard while three possible clues waited nearby.

Which clue should they investigate first?

A. A square compass pointing beneath the Diamond House.

B. Sparkling portal dust leading toward the Blue Lake.

C. A villager’s lantern blinking a strange redstone rhythm.

Chapter 1 — The Dust That Remembered

Bear chose the portal dust.

Papa packed torches, a glass sample bottle, a coil of rope, and their portable crafting table. Sentinel waited by the gate in gleaming gold armor.

“Ready?” Papa asked.

Bear looked once at the silent obsidian doorway. His tummy felt jumpy, but his hands stayed steady. “Ready.”

The dust glittered across the grass in tiny square flecks. It climbed a dirt block, crossed the farm path, and slipped beneath the dark trees.

They followed slowly. Papa placed torches as they went, making a bright path back to the Diamond House. Sentinel walked ahead, stopping at every bend until Bear and Papa caught up.

At the ravine, the portal dust sparkled down the old stone stairway.

Far below, the Blue Lake glowed like moonlight trapped under glass.

Bear held the rope rail while Papa followed one step behind. “The dust is not falling,” Bear noticed. “It is choosing blocks.”

“Or responding to something in them,” Papa said.

On the shore, the trail passed the diamond armor stands and ended at a flat wall Bear had never noticed before. Four square frames were carved into it. Three held pictures: the Diamond House, the Blue Lake, and an obsidian doorway. The fourth frame was empty.

Sentinel leaned close. A hidden redstone lamp blinked beneath the blank square.

Papa caught one dust fleck in the glass bottle. The four frames lit. For one second, glowing lines drew the deck, the windows, and the warm roof of Papa Bear Home.

Then the picture crumbled into sparkles.

“It remembers home,” Bear whispered.

“But it cannot hold the route,” Papa said. “The empty frame may need an anchor—something that means home clearly enough to guide the portal.”

Bear looked at the waiting square. They still did not know the way out, but now they knew what was missing.

When they learn how the anchor works, what should Bear use?

A. Remember the deck lights and blink their red-blue-green pattern into the map.

B. Use his telescope and align the map with the Shepherd Star.

C. Copy Papa’s familiar three-knock rhythm onto a redstone plate.

Chapter 2 — The Four-Square Map

The picture was gone, but a few sparks still crawled around the empty frame.

Papa brushed blue moss from the wall. Tiny letters appeared beneath the four squares: PORTAL-ROUTE MAP — TEST BEFORE TRAVEL.

“An old map with instructions,” Bear said. “That is my favorite kind of old thing.”

A stone drawer clicked open. Inside were a redstone comparator, three repeaters, a lever, and a square of blue wool. Papa set the comparator beside the wall.

“This can measure a redstone signal,” he explained. “Not just whether one exists, but whether it stays strong.”

Sentinel’s gold armor flashed as he turned toward a little stone island in the Blue Lake. A blue-wool flag stood there. Between the shore and the island, one glass bridge block was missing. The water below was shallow and still, but the gap was too wide to step across.

Bear examined the four-square map. “Maybe it has a practice route.”

He placed the loose blue-wool square into the empty frame. At once, the carved picture copied the island’s blue flag. A thin glass block shimmered over the gap—then vanished.

“The map recognized the place,” Papa said, “but the route did not stay.”

Bear checked the comparator. Its front torch had blinked once and gone dark. “The button signal was only a quick pulse.”

He connected the lever through a repeater. When he switched it on, the comparator glowed without flickering. The missing glass block returned and stayed bright.

Sentinel walked across first. He stood on the island, silent and steady, while Bear tested the bridge with one boot. Solid. Bear crossed, and Papa followed right behind.

On the island, Bear lifted the flag, then put it back exactly where it belonged. The map’s fourth picture remained clear.

“So the empty frame needs two things,” Bear said. “A picture it can truly recognize—”

“—and a route signal that stays steady long enough to guide the way,” Papa finished.

Bear looked across the lake at the map. This little test route only crossed a few blocks, but it had worked. A home route would need a recognizable home anchor and the same kind of steady signal. They had not chosen the anchor yet. They did not need to. First, they could learn how to keep any route from wobbling.

Papa gathered the spare redstone pieces. “Good experiment.”

Bear grinned. “Good tiny bridge.”

If they eventually make the home route appear but it starts wobbling, which safe backup should they prepare?

A. A repeater line, placing one repeater every four blocks to refresh the fading signal.

B. A comparator memory loop, feeding the same signal strength back into the map until everyone is across.

C. A lever-lock box, sliding a redstone block into contact to hold the route steadily on.

Chapter 3 — Knock, Knock, Home

Bear carried the blue-wool square back across the little glass bridge.

“I know our home anchor,” he said. “Your three knocks.”

Papa smiled. Whenever he came to Bear’s bedroom door, he used the same rhythm: knock-knock… knock. Two close together. One after a gentle pause.

“That rhythm means you,” Bear said. “And you mean home.”

They set the portable crafting table beside the four-square map. Bear laid smooth stone across it like a flat plate. Papa added redstone dust, three note blocks, and repeaters that could control the spaces between sounds.

Sentinel brought a wooden button from the supply drawer and placed it beside Bear’s hand.

Bear pressed it.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

All three sounds came out evenly. Portal dust flashed, but the empty frame showed only a jumble of square windows.

“Close,” Papa said. “Not familiar yet.”

Bear tried moving one repeater. The next rhythm went knock… knock-knock.

The map drew the Minecraft farm.

Again. Knock-knock-knock.

The map drew the Diamond House.

Again. Knock… knock… knock.

The pictures blurred together.

The redstone lamps began blinking faster. The wall showed the farm, the ravine, the Blue Lake, and the Diamond House all at once. Bright square paths crossed over one another like tangled rails.

Bear’s chest tightened. “There are too many ways.”

Papa did not touch the circuit. He sat on the stone beside Bear and placed both hands loosely on his knees.

“In,” Papa said softly.

Bear breathed in.

“Out.”

They breathed out together while Sentinel stood between them and the flashing wall.

Papa tapped the true rhythm against his knee. Tap-tap… tap.

Bear listened instead of looking. The first two knocks were almost touching. The pause before the third was longer than he had set it.

“I was adjusting the sounds,” Bear said. “But the important part is the quiet between them.”

He turned the first repeater to its shortest delay. He turned the second to a longer delay. Then he drew one clean redstone line from the button to the plate.

“Ready to test?” Papa asked.

Bear nodded and pressed.

Knock-knock… knock.

The tangled pictures vanished.

Portal dust rose from the glass bottle in a gentle sparkling ribbon. It settled into the empty fourth frame. First came the familiar deck. Then the warm windows. Then the roof of Papa Bear Home beneath the ordinary round moon.

The picture stayed.

“It knows the anchor,” Bear whispered.

The four carved squares folded inward like blocks in a puzzle. A purple doorway opened in the wall, quiet and steady enough to show the deck on the other side. Bear could see the patio and the perfectly still little bear statues waiting where they belonged.

Papa checked the comparator. “Correct destination.”

Bear checked the route line. “No crossed paths.”

Sentinel stepped near the doorway, and the picture did not change.

They had done it. They knew how to get home.

Then the comparator dropped by one tiny mark before rising again.

“A wobble,” Bear said.

“A small one,” Papa agreed. “The anchor is correct. Before we travel, we use the safe backup we prepared.”

Bear looked through the doorway, relieved rather than rushed. Home was there. It was not disappearing. They could take one careful step at a time.

When Bear is safely home, how should he remember the way they found it?

A. Draw the four-square map in his blue notebook and tape one harmless portal-dust fleck beside it.

B. Build a tiny redstone doorway model and make its lamp blink the three-knock rhythm.

C. Place three smooth blocks on the deck, tap the home rhythm, and share cocoa with Papa.

Chapter 4 — The Block That Held the Door

Bear lifted the redstone block from the map’s supply drawer.

It was bright red, perfectly square, and heavier than it looked. He carried it with both hands against his chest while Papa built a low stone box beside the route line.

The box had a slot in the top, a contact plate at the bottom, and a release lever on Sentinel’s side.

“When the block touches the plate, it will hold the signal on,” Papa said. “No blinking. No fading.”

“And after we cross?” Bear asked.

Papa pointed to the release lever. “Sentinel waits for our three-knock signal. Then he releases the block and the doorway closes safely.”

Sentinel stood taller in his gold armor. He placed one square foot beside the lever.

The purple doorway gave another wobble. Papa Bear Home stretched sideways, snapped back, and stretched again. The comparator dropped two marks.

“Not safe to cross yet,” Bear said.

“Correct,” Papa replied. “Anchor first. Backup second. Travel third.”

Bear stepped carefully to the lock box. The block tugged at his arms.

“Want help?” Papa asked.

“I can put it down,” Bear said. “Please guide the corner.”

Papa steadied one edge without taking the weight. Bear bent his knees and lowered the redstone block.

Thunk.

The block settled into the slot.

Every redstone lamp around the four-square map shone at the same steady brightness. The comparator rose to its top mark and stayed there. The purple doorway became square and clear. On the other side, the deck boards no longer rippled.

Papa clipped a rope to a blue-wool test bundle and slid it through. Bear watched the bundle land on the deck. Papa pulled it back. Not one thread had changed.

“Route test passed,” Bear said.

Then came the hard part: saying goodbye for now.

Bear rested one hand gently against Sentinel’s golden helmet. “Thank you for guarding us.”

Sentinel lowered his head beneath Bear’s hand. Then he walked to the release lever and waited.

Bear faced the doorway. Papa stood close behind him.

“One careful step,” Papa said.

Bear stepped through.

Cool Minecraft stone became the warm wooden deck beneath his feet. Little Polar Bear and Little Red Bear remained perfectly still on the patio, exactly where they belonged.

Behind him, the portal flickered once as a blue wave rolled across the underground lake. The lever-lock held. The doorway stayed open.

Papa stepped through beside Bear.

Both of them were home.

Papa turned and tapped the obsidian edge.

Knock-knock… knock.

Through the doorway, Sentinel nudged the release lever. The redstone block lifted away from its contact plate. The purple square folded inward until only three soft sparks remained. Then the obsidian frame faded from the deck, leaving ordinary evening air.

Bear let out one long breath.

Papa knelt beside him. “Where are we?”

“Home,” Bear said.

“And are we both safe?”

Bear checked the deck, the patio, and Papa beside him. “Both safe.”

A single harmless portal-dust fleck twinkled on Bear’s sleeve. He brushed it into the glass sample bottle and carried it inside. The way home was no longer missing. They had found it carefully, tested it, and closed it behind them.

Chapter 5 — Bear’s Own Ending

Inside Papa Bear Home, Bear set the glass bottle safely on the table.

“I don’t want the notebook choice,” he said. “Or the little doorway. I want to do my own thing.”

Papa smiled. “Tell me your plan.”

“I carry the redstone block. I put it down. Then I jump down into the portal.”

“The real block stayed with Sentinel,” Papa said. “And the real portal is closed.”

“I know. We make ours.”

That was a very Bear plan.

They spread Bear’s purple quilt across the living-room rug. That became the portal. Papa placed one thick floor cushion beside it—low enough for a safe hop. Bear chose a red cube from his building bin to be the redstone block.

He carried it with both hands, just as he had beside the Blue Lake.

“The route is wobbling!” Papa announced.

Bear lowered the cube onto a cardboard lock box.

Thunk.

“Signal steady,” Bear said.

Then he climbed onto the floor cushion. Papa stood close, though Bear did not need help.

“Anchor first,” Bear said.

He tapped the box. Knock-knock… knock.

“Backup second.” He checked the red cube.

“Travel third!”

Bear hopped down into the soft purple quilt. The pretend portal went fwump, which was the sound Bear decided portals made when they were being cooperative.

Papa landed one careful foot beside him. “Home again.”

They laughed, folded the quilt, and returned the red cube to its bin.

Later, Papa tucked Bear into bed. He stepped into the hallway, then tapped the doorframe.

Knock-knock… knock.

Bear’s eyes grew sleepy. The rhythm no longer meant a missing way home. It meant Papa was nearby, the adventure was finished, and both of them were exactly where they belonged.

“Goodnight, Papa.”

“Goodnight, Bear.”

Papa Bear Home settled into its quiet nighttime sounds, safe and warm.
