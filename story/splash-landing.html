Splash Landing

Cheetah and Bear spend two days spying on Papa's porch routine and rig a Minecraft-inspired sticky-piston water balloon drop — then adapt on the fly when Papa shifts position at the last second.

THWUMP.

Cheetah landed in the yard with her tail swishing extra fast, both paws wrapped around a lumpy, dripping bag.

“Bear,” she whispered, golden eyes sparkling with mischief, “I have a plan. A very good plan.”

Bear peeked into the bag. Water balloons — dozens of them, wobbling and cool.

“For Papa?” he whispered back.

Cheetah grinned. “Right over the door. Right when he walks out. He will never see it coming.”

Bear glanced toward the porch, where Papa was humming over his morning coffee, completely unaware.

This was going to be the best secret of the whole summer.

Chapter 1 — Spy Training

“Okay,” Cheetah whispered, flopping down next to Bear behind the porch railing. “Rule one of the plan.”

“Rule one,” Bear whispered back, grinning so hard his cheeks hurt.

“We are spies now.”

Bear snorted, and Cheetah clapped a paw over his mouth, both of them shaking with silent laughter.

“Shh! Spies don't laugh!”

“Spies definitely laugh,” Bear whispered. “Secret laughing is a spy skill.”

They peeked over the railing. Papa was still on the porch, humming, turning the pages of his newspaper one slow page at a time.

“We need to know exactly when he's going to walk under the door,” Cheetah said, golden eyes narrowed like she was solving the most important puzzle in the world. “Not too early. Not too late. Exactly right.”

“So we watch him,” Bear said.

“We watch him,” Cheetah agreed. “Every morning. We learn his pattern. Coffee, paper, stretch, door.”

Bear pulled out his blue notebook and flipped to a fresh page. At the top he wrote, in his best secret-mission handwriting: PAPA WATCH — DAY ONE.

“Coffee,” Bear murmured, watching Papa take a sip. He wrote it down.

“Paper,” Cheetah whispered, ears twitching as Papa turned a page.

They watched him fold the newspaper closed, set down his mug, and push both paws against the porch rail in one long, satisfied stretch — the stretch he always did right before standing up.

“There it is,” Cheetah breathed. “The stretch. That's the signal. Every single day.”

Bear wrote STRETCH = GO TIME in big letters and underlined it twice.

Papa stood, yawned, and wandered back inside without noticing two very small spies grinning at each other behind the railing.

“Tomorrow,” Cheetah whispered, “we time it exactly. And the day after that —” she wiggled her eyebrows — “we build the machine.”

Bear hugged his notebook to his chest, trying not to giggle out loud.

This was, without question, the funniest secret he had ever kept.

Chapter 2 — Watching Papa's Pattern

The next morning, Bear and Cheetah watched from behind the railing again, both of them holding their breath.

Papa took a sip of coffee. Bear wrote COFFEE, 7:12.

Papa turned a page. Cheetah's tail twitched, counting silently, one paw tapping out each second against the porch boards.

Then Papa folded his paper, set down his mug, and pushed both paws against the rail for the big stretch.

“STRETCH!” Bear whispered, and Cheetah slapped a paw over his mouth just in time to catch his giggle before it escaped.

They watched Papa amble to the door, hand on the handle, and disappear inside, humming the same three notes he always hummed.

Bear scribbled in his notebook. “Same as yesterday. Coffee. Paper. Stretch. Door. Every single time.”

“Every time,” Cheetah repeated, grinning so wide her whiskers nearly touched her ears. “Which means tomorrow, we know exactly when the door opens before it opens.”

They spent the rest of the morning double-checking, sneaking looks at the porch every few minutes just in case Papa broke his pattern. He never did. He watered the tomatoes at the same time. He waved to the mail carrier at the same time. He hummed the same silly tune, note for note.

“He's very reliable,” Cheetah said approvingly, tapping her chin with one paw like a detective closing a case. “Excellent subject for a prank. Truly excellent.”

Bear laughed so hard he had to lie down flat in the grass, arms and legs starfished out, staring up at the clouds and shaking with the effort of staying quiet.

Cheetah flopped down next to him, both of them cackling in whispers at the sky.

That evening, after Papa had gone inside for good, Bear and Cheetah sat on the back steps comparing notes one last time, the sky turning soft orange above them, fireflies just starting to blink on near the fence.

“Tomorrow we build,” Cheetah said, bumping her shoulder gently against Bear's. “The stretch is the signal. Reliable as sunrise. We just need something that catches the balloons and lets them go the second he stretches — no hands required, no waiting around and giving ourselves away.”

“Something automatic,” Bear said, eyes lighting up like he'd just found the missing piece of a puzzle.

“Something automatic,” Cheetah agreed, golden eyes gleaming with the particular joy of a plan finally coming together. “I might know exactly the thing.”

“Minecraft thing?” Bear guessed, already grinning.

“Minecraft thing,” Cheetah confirmed proudly.

They shook paw and hand on it right there on the steps, trying very hard — and mostly failing completely — not to giggle themselves silly under the darkening sky.

Chapter 3 — Sticky Piston Splash

The garage was Bear's favorite kind of mess — bins of gears, a coil of rope, and Papa's old redstone-inspired toy set that clicked and whirred when you pushed the right buttons.

“Okay,” Cheetah said, spreading everything out on the floor like a general planning a battle. “In Minecraft, when you want something to hold still and then let go all at once, you use a sticky piston. It pushes a block out, and when you take away the signal, it pulls the block back — but only if you tell it exactly when. That's the whole trick. Hold. Wait. Release.”

“So we need our own sticky piston,” Bear said, already digging through the bin of parts.

“We need our own sticky piston,” Cheetah agreed, sorting pieces into three careful piles. “Rope loop for the hold, holding board for the balance, quick-release pin for the exact moment.”

Bear held the bucket steady — an old mixing bowl lined with a trash bag, balloons piled in gently so none of them would pop early — while Cheetah threaded a length of rope through two hooks above the porch door, exactly where Papa always stood mid-stretch.

“The rope holds the bucket up,” Cheetah explained, tying a careful loop and testing its weight with one paw. “The pin holds the rope in place. Pull the pin, the rope lets go, the bucket tips — splash, right on cue.”

“And the pin is our button,” Bear said, tracing the string that ran from the pin all the way down behind the flowerpot, right where he and Cheetah would be hiding tomorrow morning.

They tested it three times with a bucket of nothing but air, watching it swing and tip exactly the way they wanted, timing each pull against an imaginary Papa-stretch.

“Again,” Cheetah said, resetting the bucket. “We only get one real try. It has to work the first time.”

On the fourth test, Cheetah tugged the string a half-second early and the bucket wobbled without tipping.

“Needs a stronger pin,” she muttered, adjusting the notch and testing the tension with two paws. “Sticky pistons don't wobble halfway. They commit completely, or not at all.”

Bear found a slightly thicker dowel in the bin and they swapped it in. This time, when Cheetah pulled, the whole bucket tipped clean and fast, and would have soaked the garage floor if it had held anything but air.

“That's it,” Bear whispered, grinning. “That's the one.”

They ran the test five more times just to be sure — resetting, pulling, watching the tip, resetting again — until the motion felt as natural and certain as a held breath finally let go.

By the time the sun dipped low and orange again, the bucket hung steady and silent above the door, balanced exactly right, string trailing down to their hiding spot behind the flowerpot, ready and waiting.

“Tomorrow,” Bear whispered, patting the string like it was a pet, “Papa stretches, we pull, and —”

“Splash,” Cheetah finished, eyes shining with barely contained glee. “A perfect, sticky-piston splash.”

“Do you think he'll laugh?” Bear asked, suddenly a little nervous underneath all the giggles.

Cheetah considered this seriously, tail swishing. “Papa always laughs. Even at himself. Especially at himself.”

That settled something warm and happy in Bear's chest, right alongside the excitement.

They high-fived so quietly it barely made a sound, checked the string one final time, and crept off to bed still grinning about tomorrow's surprise.

Before bed, Bear tiptoed back out just to check the string one more time, flashlight cupped low so the beam wouldn't wander toward the house. Cheetah met him there, already checking the same knot from the other side.

“Can't sleep either?” Bear whispered.

“Too excited,” Cheetah admitted. “What if he steps under it at the wrong angle? What if the balloons are too heavy and the rope snaps early?”

Bear thought about it, running his fingers along the taut line. “Then we watch really closely tomorrow. And if something looks wrong, we don't pull.”

Cheetah nodded slowly, some of the worry easing out of her shoulders. “Right. We only pull if it's exactly right. Sticky pistons don't fire on maybe. They fire on certain.”

“Fire on certain,” Bear repeated, and it made them both laugh quietly into their paws and hands.

They gave the string one last gentle tug — testing only, not triggering — and satisfied, padded back inside, past the sleeping house, toward two very excited pillows.

Chapter 4 — The Backup Plan

The next morning arrived bright and warm, and Bear woke up before his alarm, heart already thumping with excitement.

He met Cheetah behind the flowerpot exactly on schedule. She was crouched low, tail curled tight around her paws, eyes fixed on the porch door.

“Position check,” she whispered.

“Position check,” Bear whispered back, gripping the string just like they'd practiced. “Bucket's full. String's tight. We're ready.”

Right on time, Papa stepped onto the porch with his coffee, newspaper tucked under one arm, humming his same three notes.

Bear and Cheetah exchanged a look — the look of two spies about to pull off the perfect mission.

Papa sipped his coffee. Turned a page. Sipped again.

Then, without warning, a gust of wind rattled through the yard, and the porch door creaked — Papa reached out and pulled it half-shut against the breeze, shifting his whole stretch two full steps to the left.

Bear's stomach dropped. “He moved!”

“I see it,” Cheetah whispered back, completely calm, already adjusting the string in her paws. “We tested for this. Remember? We built in slack.”

Because they'd prepared ahead of time — three extra feet of rope coiled loosely by the flowerpot exactly for a moment like this — Cheetah simply fed out a little more line, letting the bucket's anchor point shift with it, smooth as anything they'd rehearsed.

“Steady,” Cheetah breathed, eyes never leaving Papa. “Steady... steady...”

Papa folded his newspaper, set down his mug, and pushed both paws against the rail for the stretch, right in his new spot.

“Now,” Cheetah whispered.

Bear pulled the string.

The bucket tipped clean and fast — just like all their practice runs — and a rainbow of water balloons rained down, bursting in a glorious, splashing cascade right over Papa's head.

Papa gasped, dropped his newspaper, and stood there dripping from ear to ear, utterly soaked from head to paw.

For one heart-stopping second, nobody moved. Bear held his breath.

Then Papa started to laugh — a big, booming, delighted laugh that shook his whole chest — and Bear and Cheetah tumbled out from behind the flowerpot, laughing so hard they could barely stand up straight.

“You got me!” Papa said, wiping water from his eyes, grinning ear to ear. “You really, truly got me good!”

“We had a backup plan!” Bear announced proudly, still catching his breath. “In case you moved off your spot!”

Papa raised his eyebrows, genuinely impressed, water still dripping from his chin onto his soaked shirt. “You planned for a backup plan?”

“Sticky pistons always have slack,” Cheetah said solemnly, tail swishing with pride, as if this explained absolutely everything in the world.

Papa laughed even harder at that, shaking his head and wringing out his shirt with both paws. “Remind me never to underestimate you two again.”

Chapter 5 — Cocoa and Confessions

By the time Papa had toweled off and changed into a dry shirt, the whole yard smelled like summer and wet grass, and the porch floor was scattered with soggy, colorful balloon scraps.

He came back out with three mugs of hot cocoa balanced carefully in his paws, and handed two down to Bear and Cheetah, who were still giggling on the steps.

“So,” Papa said, settling down beside them, eyes twinkling, “tell me everything. From the very beginning.”

Bear lit up. “We watched you for two whole days! We knew exactly when you'd stretch!”

“And we built a sticky piston,” Cheetah added proudly. “A real one. With a rope and a pin and everything.”

Papa raised his eyebrows, thoroughly impressed. “A sticky piston. In real life.”

“It needed slack,” Bear said, “because you moved. But we planned for that too.”

Papa looked between the two of them, shaking his head with a warm, amazed smile. “You two really did think of everything.”

“We practiced,” Cheetah said simply. “A lot.”

“That's exactly it,” Papa said, wrapping one arm around Bear and giving Cheetah's ear a gentle scratch. “The best surprises come from careful thinking, not just luck. You two didn't just get me wet — you built something that actually worked, and you were ready when things changed. That's real engineering.”

Bear beamed, cocoa warm in his hands, the whole world feeling soft and golden in the evening light.

“Can we do it again sometime?” Bear asked, already thinking ahead.

Papa laughed. “Let me dry off first. But yes — someday. Fair warning, though: I might just build my own sticky piston next time.”

Cheetah gasped in delighted mock horror. “The student becomes the teacher.”

They all laughed together, the sound settling warm over the porch as the sky faded to soft evening blue.

Eventually, cocoa finished and yawns arriving one after another, Bear said goodnight to Cheetah at the door, and Papa carried him up to bed, still smiling about backup plans and sticky pistons and the best kind of getting-caught.

“Goodnight, silly spy,” Papa whispered, tucking the blanket in close.

“Goodnight, Papa,” Bear murmured, already halfway to sleep, dreaming of tomorrow's next great plan.
