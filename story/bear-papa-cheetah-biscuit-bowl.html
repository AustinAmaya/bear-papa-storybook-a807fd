Bear, Papa, and Cheetah at the Bottom of the Ocean

A cosy ocean-floor research adventure with a careful rover rescue and an octopus who is definitely not a napkin.

Preamble

THWUMP.

Cheetah landed beside the deck with a small overnight bag.

“I have packed for the bottom of the ocean,” she announced.

Bear looked inside.

“A towel?”

“Optimism.”

Papa came out carrying three travel tickets and a picture of a research station. It stood on the seabed like a cluster of round, glowing cottages.

“We'll stay dry inside,” he said.

Cheetah tucked the towel away.

“Backup optimism.”

Bear studied the picture. Beyond one window, a little research rover waited on the sand.

“Can we drive that?”

Papa smiled.

“That is exactly what we're going to learn.”

Chapter 1 — Down Is a Direction

At the harbour, Bear inspected their little passenger submarine.

It had padded seats, thick round windows, and a yellow stripe around its middle.

“It looks like a lemon with a job,” Cheetah said.

“A very important lemon,” Bear agreed.

Papa checked their bags while the pilot welcomed them aboard. Bear fastened his own seat belt, then gave it a firm tug. Cheetah sat beside him. Papa took the seat on his other side.

The submarine slipped beneath the surface.

Green water washed across the windows.

Bear watched a silver fish turn, showing its bright side like a coin. Farther down, the green became blue. Farther still, the blue grew dark.

“Where did the sunshine go?” Bear asked.

“Water gets in its way,” Papa said. “Down here, we bring our own lights.”

Cheetah leaned close to the window.

“I can see my face.”

“You've discovered a very deep cheetah,” Bear said.

Then the submarine's lamps caught pale sand below them.

There was the station.

Its round rooms stood on stout legs. Short tunnels joined them. A small rover rested outside a broad observation window, its camera raised on a stalk.

The submarine docked with a gentle clunk.

They waited while the pilot checked the connection. Only when the green light came on did the inner door open into a dry passage.

Bear stepped through.

There were coat hooks.

There were slippers.

There was a notice saying PLEASE KEEP CRUMBS OUT OF THE CONTROLS.

“Proper science,” Papa said, reading it.

A researcher showed them the visitor room and the rover console. Tonight's project was simple: photograph the same little patch of seabed twice, to see what changed.

Bear chose a flat rock beside a shallow sandy hollow.

Cheetah pointed at the picture.

“The Biscuit Bowl.”

“It doesn't have biscuits,” Bear said.

“Early days.”

The researcher showed Papa the controls, then stayed nearby at the next desk. Papa would supervise their turn.

Bear settled into the chair.

On the screen, the little rover faced the enormous, quiet ocean.

“Ready?” Papa asked.

Bear put his hands beside the controls.

“Let's visit the bowl.”

Chapter 2 — The Snow That Wasn't Snow

The rover rolled forward.

Bear nudged the control again.

On the screen, the seabed moved past much faster than he expected.

“Oop.”

He let go.

The rover stopped.

Papa pointed to the practice marker, a striped post beside the station.

“Try going just as far as that.”

Bear pressed gently.

This time the rover crept along. Cheetah watched the distance marks on the screen.

“Nearly. Nearly. There.”

Bear released the control.

The post stood neatly in the middle of the picture.

“Small moves,” he said.

“Excellent parking,” Cheetah said. “Not one biscuit injured.”

Before going farther, they tried the rover's tools. A small gripper opened and closed. Its camera tilted up and down. A bracket carried a spare measuring marker, shaped like a white letter T.

Bear used the gripper to lift the marker from its bracket and put it back.

The first time, he missed.

The second time, he watched the shadow beneath it and lined it up properly.

Click.

“Useful practice,” Papa said.

Then they drove to the flat rock.

Tiny pale bits drifted through the lamps.

“Snow?” Bear asked.

“Little bits sinking from above,” Papa said. “People call it marine snow.”

Cheetah looked pleased.

“Underwater snow. Dry slippers. This place is showing off.”

Bear took a photograph of the hollow. A small crab stood at its edge with one claw raised.

“Hello,” Bear said.

The crab did not reply.

“It might be doing important crab work,” Papa suggested.

They waited while the crab walked out of the picture. Then Bear took another photograph and saved both.

Next, Papa helped him set the measuring marker on clear sand beside the rock, well away from the crab. They would use it to aim the next photograph from the same place.

A little current brushed through the hollow.

The loose sand lifted.

“That's thicker snow,” Cheetah said.

But it was coming from below.

Within moments, a pale cloud filled the screen. The rock disappeared. So did the marker.

Bear released the control.

The picture showed almost nothing.

“Did we lose the bowl?”

Papa checked the station display. The rover was connected and answering normally.

“The bowl hasn't moved. We just can't see it.”

Outside the real window, the station lights still shone steadily over the sand.

Cheetah's ears went still.

She studied the screen, then the window, then the screen again.

“Maybe,” she said, “we need a better look before we make another move.”

Chapter 3 — The Right Thing to Move

Bear wanted to turn the rover around.

Or back it up.

Or drive forward until the sand stopped being everywhere.

His hands hovered over the controls.

Papa kept his own hands still.

“First, let's be comfortable not moving.”

He breathed out slowly, as though cooling a spoonful of soup.

Bear copied him.

Cheetah settled beside the desk.

On the screen, pale grains drifted past. The rover's little status light stayed green.

“It's safe where it is,” Papa said. “We have time.”

Bear put both hands flat on his knees.

They watched.

Gradually, a dark edge appeared through the cloud.

“The rock?” Bear asked.

“Could be,” said Papa. “What would help us check?”

Bear opened their first photograph in the corner of the screen.

The rock had a notch on top, like a tiny missing bite.

He looked at the live picture.

There was the notch.

“The Biscuit Bowl!”

“I knew there'd been a biscuit,” Cheetah said.

Bear smiled. His shoulders dropped.

He moved the camera slowly down.

The measuring marker was lying on its side. One end rested beneath the rover's front wheel.

“Oh. I put it too near the slope.”

“The current shifted it,” Papa said. “Now we know that spot isn't steady enough.”

Bear tried the gentlest forward nudge.

The wheel turned, but the rover barely moved. More sand puffed up.

He stopped immediately.

“No more wheel,” he said.

“Good catch,” Papa replied.

Cheetah pointed to the screen.

“Could the gripper pick it up?”

Bear opened the gripper and reached toward the marker.

Its fingers touched the white stem.

He tried lifting.

The marker did not lift.

Bear stopped again.

“It won't come.”

Papa leaned closer without touching the controls.

“Show me where the wheel is.”

Bear pointed.

“And where's the marker?”

“Under the wheel.”

Papa waited.

Bear looked from one to the other.

“I'm trying to lift the bit we're sitting on.”

Cheetah rested her chin beside the desk.

“Like trying to pick up a rug while Papa's chair is on it.”

“An extremely handsome obstacle,” Papa said.

Bear laughed once, then studied the view.

“We need to get the wheel off first.”

Papa checked the rover's rear camera. Behind it lay clear, flat sand. He asked the researcher to confirm their plan. She looked at the displays and gave them permission for one tiny backward move.

“Cheetah, watch the wheel,” Bear said. “Tell me when it's clear.”

“I'll watch the back,” Papa said.

Bear rested one hand on the control.

“Everyone ready?”

“Ready.”

“Ready.”

He eased the rover backward.

The picture shifted just a little.

“Still touching,” Cheetah said.

Bear paused.

They let the stirred sand settle.

Then he tried another small move.

“Clear,” Cheetah said.

Bear stopped.

The white marker lay fully in view.

He reached with the gripper again. This time its fingers closed around the stem and lifted it smoothly from the sand.

“Got it!”

Cheetah raised both arms, then remembered the nearby controls and celebrated with a very small wiggle instead.

“A desk-sized cheer,” Papa said.

Bear brought the marker to its bracket.

He nearly pushed too quickly. Then he remembered the practice post and watched the little shadow.

Left a bit.

Down a bit.

Click.

The marker sat securely in place.

The researcher checked the camera picture and smiled. The rover was fine. Nothing had been left on the seabed.

Bear sat back.

“We can go home now.”

“We can,” Papa said. “Would you like one last picture first?”

Bear looked at the rock. The sand cloud had thinned. The hollow was visible again.

Instead of setting the marker down, they used the notch in the rock to line up the view. Papa saved that position for the researchers to use later.

Bear took the photograph.

In one corner, the little crab had returned.

It stood beside the hollow, claw raised.

Cheetah leaned toward the screen.

“Thank you. We agree. Very good parking.”

Bear's laugh filled the little control room.

He turned the rover slowly toward the station, with Papa checking the route and Cheetah watching beside him.

This time, the wheels left only a soft, narrow trail behind them.

Chapter 4 — The Scientist with the Napkin

The rover came home to its charging pad.

Bear checked its camera. Papa checked the dock light. Cheetah read their list aloud.

“Rover parked. Marker put away. Photographs saved. Biscuit not found.”

“That last one wasn't on the list,” Bear said.

“It was on mine.”

The researcher thanked them and brought three little bowls of warm soup to the visitor table. Beside it stood the broad observation window.

Bear carried his bowl carefully with both hands.

Beyond the thick window, the sea was dark and peaceful. The station's lamps lit a patch of sand. Farther away, little lights blinked and drifted in the water.

“Some animals make their own light,” Papa said.

“Like bringing a torch from inside you?” Bear asked.

“Rather like that.”

Cheetah pulled her chair close to the window.

“I would like glow-in-the-dark spots.”

“You'd be very easy to find,” Bear said.

“I usually am.”

She gave a tiny THWUMP with her feet beneath the chair.

Then Bear noticed something outside.

A white shape flickered beside the station's low camera.

It vanished.

It appeared again.

“Papa.”

Papa followed his pointing hand.

“Could that be something glowing?”

“Let's look.”

They left their soup safely on the table and went back to the screen. The researcher switched to the station's fixed camera.

A white shape filled half the view.

It waved.

Cheetah leaned forward.

“An underwater handkerchief.”

Bear frowned.

“Did we drop a napkin?”

He turned to count the ones on the table.

Three napkins. All present.

Papa said nothing yet. He brought up the second camera, which looked across the same spot from the side.

Bear studied both pictures.

In one, the white shape seemed enormous.

In the other, it was small and close to the lens.

“Oh! It's not a giant.”

“What else can you see?” Papa asked.

Bear looked carefully.

There was a rounded body.

There were arms.

One arm rested along the camera housing while the others reached across the sand.

“A little octopus!”

Its pale underside flashed as it moved, making it look like fluttering cloth.

Cheetah put a hand over her mouth.

“We nearly asked an octopus to wipe our faces.”

Bear laughed so hard he had to sit down.

The researcher checked the camera arm and left it still. No need to move anything while the visitor explored.

They watched quietly.

The octopus touched the housing, released it, and travelled onward over the sand. The view cleared all by itself.

Bear saved a picture with the researcher's permission.

Under it, he wrote OCTOPUS.

Cheetah added, NOT A NAPKIN.

Papa considered the page.

“A useful scientific distinction.”

Back at the table, their soup was still warm.

Bear unfolded his real napkin and inspected it.

“No arms,” he reported.

“Excellent,” Papa said. “We can eat in peace.”

Chapter 5 — All the Way Home

Later, the submarine carried them upward.

The station became a little cluster of warm windows below. Then the water hid it, and Bear watched the darkness slowly turn blue.

Cheetah sat beside him with the photograph on her lap.

“I liked the octopus,” she said.

“I liked getting the marker back.”

Papa nodded. “You stopped when the wheels stirred up sand. That gave us a chance to see what needed moving.”

Bear looked at his hands.

“They wanted to press everything.”

“Mine have wanted that too,” Papa said.

Cheetah held up her overnight bag.

“And I never used the towel.”

“Successful optimism,” Bear said.

By the time they reached Papa Bear Home, the sky was soft and dark above the deck.

They put their coats on the hooks. Papa warmed milk. Bear spread the photographs on the kitchen table while Cheetah chose her favourite.

Not the dramatic cloud of sand.

Not the close-up of the gripper.

She chose the little station, all its windows glowing on the quiet seabed.

“It looks cosy,” she said.

“It was,” Bear answered.

Papa sat between them.

For a little while, they drank and looked at the pictures. Nobody had to steer anything. Nobody had to count a list.

When Bear's eyes grew heavy, Cheetah tucked the photographs safely into his notebook and said goodnight. Papa walked her to the door, then came back to help Bear settle into bed.

The blanket was warm beneath his chin.

“Papa?”

“Yes?”

“Tomorrow's napkins should stay ordinary.”

Papa smoothed the blanket.

“I shall inspect them.”

Bear smiled.

The house was still. Papa was near.

And far below the ocean, the little research station shone peacefully over the sand.
