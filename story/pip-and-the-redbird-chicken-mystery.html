Pip and the Redbird Chicken Mystery

At Redbird, Bear and Papa find tiny pixel creeper Pip taking square bites of chicken to solve a confused portal-pixel snack signal.

6 parts, played one after another — just press play.

Status: COMPLETE

Preamble

Bear and Papa were at Redbird, sitting in a booth that smelled like warm chicken, toast, and a little bit of pepper.

Bear had just picked up one crunchy piece when he stopped.

There, on top of the chicken, stood a tiny green creeper made of pixels.

He was no taller than Bear’s thumb. His square feet went tip-tip-tip across the plate. Then he leaned down and took one tiny pixel bite.

Nibble.

A perfect little cube vanished from the chicken.

Bear blinked.

“Papa,” he whispered. “There is a creeper in our dinner.”

The creeper looked up and waved.

“Pip?” Papa said.

Pip smiled nervously. “Hello. I am being very small and mostly polite.”

Chapter 1

Bear did not yell.

That was important.

He put his chicken down slowly and folded his hands in his lap, even though his eyebrows were doing a whole gymnastics routine.

Papa leaned closer, careful not to bump the table.

“Hello, Pip,” Papa said in his calm restaurant voice. “Are you supposed to be in our food?”

Pip looked down at his tiny pixel feet, which were standing in one drop of honey sauce.

“Probably not,” he said.

Bear whispered, “Are you eating our chicken because you are hungry?”

Pip patted his square belly. “A little hungry. But mostly I am testing.”

“Testing what?” Bear asked.

Pip pointed to the missing cube in the chicken. The bite was not round like a normal bite. It was perfectly square, with tiny sparkling edges.

“The chicken is making map crumbs,” Pip said.

Papa glanced at Bear. Bear glanced at Papa.

“Map crumbs,” Bear repeated.

Pip nodded very seriously. “When I take a tiny pixel bite, the food tells me where something came from. Mostly it says, ‘kitchen.’ But this chicken says…”

He took another little bite.

Nibble.

A tiny green arrow appeared above the plate. It spun in a circle, then pointed toward the floor under the booth.

Pip’s eyes grew wide.

“There,” he said.

Bear leaned down and looked under the table. There were Bear’s shoes. Papa’s shoes. One crayon wrapper from probably somebody else. And, stuck to the metal table leg, a square red pixel the size of a pea.

It blinked.

Blink-blink.

Pip made a worried little rumble.

Papa held up one hand. “Pause. Everybody breathe.”

Bear breathed in.

Pip breathed in.

Papa breathed in.

Then out.

The rumble got softer.

“Good,” Papa said. “Now. Pip, is that pixel yours?”

Pip shuffled. “I think it came from my lunchbox portal. It must have followed the chicken smell.”

Bear remembered the Redbird adventure when Pip had needed help before. He remembered portals and Minecraft and a lot of careful not-startling.

“So the red pixel is making you take bites?” Bear asked.

“Not making,” Pip said quickly. “Suggesting. Very strongly. Like when a snack smells delicious and your feet walk that direction without a meeting.”

Papa smiled. “A snack suggestion.”

“Yes,” Pip said. “But if the pixel keeps blinking, it may call more food signals. Then all the chicken might start showing map arrows.”

Bear looked at the plate.

Another green arrow popped up from a chicken tender and pointed under the booth.

Then one appeared above Papa’s biscuit.

Papa raised one eyebrow. “That biscuit is innocent.”

Pip clasped his tiny pixel hands. “I can fix it. I just need help reaching the blinking pixel without sneezing confetti onto the restaurant floor.”

Bear nodded. “We can help.”

Papa pulled a clean napkin from the dispenser and laid it flat like a soft work mat.

“Restaurant engineering,” Papa said. “Quiet hands. Small steps. No sudden noises.”

Pip climbed onto the napkin.

Tip-tip-tip.

His tiny face looked brave and wobbly at the same time.

Bear smiled at him. “We’ve helped you before. We can do it again.”

Chapter 2

Papa made a tiny safety plan right there on the table.

He drew it on the back of the receipt with a little golf pencil from the hostess stand.

Step one: give Pip a quiet path.

Step two: catch any pixel crumbs.

Step three: find out why the red pixel came to Redbird.

Bear watched carefully. “What do we use for a quiet path?”

Papa looked around the table. “We have a napkin, two straw wrappers, a sauce cup lid, and one pickle slice.”

Bear considered the pickle slice.

“Probably not the pickle.”

“Probably not,” Papa agreed.

Pip raised one tiny hand. “I like pickles, but they are not structural.”

Bear took the two straw wrappers and laid them from the edge of the plate down to the booth seat, like little rails. Papa folded the napkin into a soft ramp. Pip tested it with one foot.

Tip.

The ramp held.

“Excellent,” Papa said. “Now we need a crumb catcher.”

Bear placed the sauce cup lid under the table leg, directly below the blinking red pixel.

Pip peered over the edge of the booth seat.

“That is a very long way down for someone thumb-sized,” he said.

Bear held out one hand, palm flat. “You can ride my hand elevator.”

Pip looked at Bear’s hand. Then he looked at Bear’s face.

“Slow elevator?”

“Very slow,” Bear said.

Pip stepped on.

Bear lowered him toward the sauce cup lid. Papa kept one hand nearby, not grabbing, just ready.

Halfway down, a server walked past carrying a tray.

Clink-clink.

Pip froze.

His little sides began to glow.

“Too much sound,” Pip whispered.

Bear stopped the elevator right away.

Papa spoke softly. “Pause point.”

Bear held still. Pip held still. Even the green arrows above the chicken seemed to hold still, which was probably not true, but it felt polite.

Papa took a clean paper menu and curved it around Bear’s hand, making a quiet little wall.

“Sound shade,” Papa said.

Pip’s glow faded.

“No sneeze,” Pip whispered proudly.

“No sneeze,” Bear whispered back.

They continued down.

When Pip reached the sauce cup lid, he stepped off and approached the blinking red pixel. Up close, it looked like a tiny redstone crumb, with a little bit of honey sauce stuck to one corner.

Pip sniffed it.

“Oh,” he said.

“What?” Bear asked.

Pip looked up. “It is not lost. It is hungry.”

Papa leaned under the booth as far as a grown-up in a chicken restaurant could lean without becoming part of the furniture.

“Hm,” he said. “Can a portal pixel be hungry?”

Pip nodded. “Portal pixels eat directions. This one followed the smell of Redbird chicken and forgot its way home.”

Bear’s eyes widened. “It needs a direction snack.”

The red pixel blinked again.

Blink-blink.

A green arrow popped out of Bear’s toast and pointed not at the floor this time, but toward the front door.

Then it flickered and pointed toward the kitchen.

Then toward the ceiling.

Pip gulped. “It is getting confused.”

Papa tapped the receipt plan. “Then we help it remember one direction at a time.”

Bear looked at the tiny red pixel, the arrows, the chicken, and Pip standing bravely on the sauce lid.

“We need a compass,” Bear said.

Pip’s square face brightened. “A chicken compass?”

“A clean one,” Papa said quickly.

Bear grinned. “A clean chicken compass.”

Chapter 3

They built the compass from things on the table.

Papa used the round sauce cup lid as the base. Bear placed four tiny torn pieces of clean napkin around the edge: one for table, one for kitchen, one for door, and one for home.

Pip carefully took one more tiny pixel bite of chicken.

Nibble.

This time Bear caught the map crumb on a napkin square.

The crumb glowed green and zipped to the sauce lid.

It pointed toward the kitchen.

“That makes sense,” Papa said. “The chicken came from the kitchen.”

Pip took a tiny bite of toast.

Nibble.

The crumb pointed toward Bear’s plate.

“That makes sense too,” Bear said. “Toast came from here.”

Then Pip touched the blinking red pixel.

The compass spun.

Kitchen.

Door.

Ceiling.

Chicken.

Door.

Door.

Door.

Pip stepped back. “It wants outside.”

Papa watched the pattern. “Or it thinks outside is the way back to Minecraft.”

Bear looked toward the front door of Redbird. People were coming in and out. A tiny pixel could get stepped on or startled.

“We can’t just let it hop away,” Bear said.

“No,” Papa said. “We guide it.”

The red pixel blinked harder. Arrows popped up above the biscuit, fries, ketchup, and even a napkin, which had never been food and looked offended.

Pip started to glow again.

“I think it is calling too loudly,” he said.

Papa lowered his voice. “Then we make the signal smaller.”

“How?” Bear asked.

Papa pointed to the compass. “Signals get messy when they have too many choices. We remove extra choices.”

Bear moved the chicken plate to one side. Papa moved the biscuit and toast farther away. Pip stayed on the sauce lid with the red pixel, whispering, “Easy signal. Easy signal.”

The green arrows faded until only one remained.

It pointed to Bear’s jacket pocket.

Bear patted it. “My pocket?”

Inside he found a little folded paper from home. It was an old drawing of Pip’s quiet corner: soft light, cozy carrier, and cucumber slices. Bear had tucked it there because he liked the drawing.

Pip’s eyes got shiny.

“My calm map,” he said.

The red pixel blinked at the drawing.

Blink.

Not blink-blink anymore.

Just one small blink.

Papa smiled. “It doesn’t need a door. It needs the way to Pip’s safe place.”

Bear put the drawing on the table beside the compass. The red pixel hopped from the sauce lid to the napkin ramp.

Hop.

Then to Bear’s hand.

Hop.

Then onto the drawing.

It settled right on the little square that showed Pip’s carrier.

Pip made a tiny relieved sound. “That is home-shaped.”

For one second, everything was calm.

Then the restaurant speaker made a cheerful ding.

“Order seventy-two!” called someone at the counter.

Pip startled.

The red pixel startled too.

Pop!

A puff of green pixel confetti burst over the table.

Not a lot.

Just enough to sprinkle Bear’s napkin, Papa’s sleeve, and one french fry.

Pip gasped. “I sneezed a little.”

The red pixel split into three smaller red pixels.

Bear stared.

Papa looked at the three pixels.

Then at the confetti.

Then at Pip.

“Well,” Papa said. “Now we have a smaller problem, three times.”

Bear giggled before he could stop himself.

Pip giggled too, which made one confetti square fall off his nose.

The three red pixels bounced on the calm map.

Hop. Hop. Hop.

One bounced toward Pip’s carrier drawing.

One bounced toward the Minecraft diamond house Bear had drawn in the corner.

One bounced toward a tiny cucumber slice.

Papa tapped the table gently. “They are not running away. They are choosing parts of home.”

Bear leaned close. “So we give each pixel the right home part.”

Pip stood tall. “I can help. I know my home parts.”

The green arrows over the food disappeared. The chicken was just chicken again. The biscuit relaxed from being suspicious.

Bear, Papa, and Pip got to work.

Chapter 4

The first tiny red pixel sat on the carrier drawing.

Pip touched it gently.

“That is my cozy place,” he said. “Dim light. Soft towel. No loud dings.”

The pixel turned from red to warm green.

Plink.

It sank into the drawing like a sticker finding its page.

The second tiny red pixel bounced on the diamond house.

“That is my Minecraft home,” Pip said. “Bright diamond walls. Square windows. Very shiny. Sometimes too shiny before breakfast.”

Bear smiled. “We know that house.”

Papa nodded. “We helped you get back there.”

The pixel blinked, as if remembering. Pip hummed a soft creeper hum.

Mmmm-mmm.

Bear hummed too, very quietly.

The second pixel turned green and settled.

Plink.

The third pixel was sitting on the cucumber slice drawing.

Pip looked embarrassed.

“That is snack,” he said.

“Snack is important,” Bear said.

“Very,” Papa agreed.

Pip touched the third pixel. “Cucumber slice. Crunchy. Calm. Good after big feelings.”

The pixel turned green.

Plink.

The drawing glowed softly. All three home parts connected with little green lines: cozy carrier, diamond house, cucumber snack.

Then the original red pixel reappeared in the middle, whole again, but not red anymore.

Green.

Calm.

Pip sighed. “It remembered home.”

Bear looked at the chicken plate. There were still a few tiny square bites missing, but not too many.

“Will it go back now?” he asked.

The green pixel floated up from the drawing.

It made a tiny square doorway in the air, no bigger than a cracker.

Inside the doorway, Bear saw blocky grass, a little diamond house, and what looked like a very excited cucumber plant.

Pip waved both tiny hands. “That is my home window!”

Papa held the menu upright to make a sound shade. Bear kept his hands still and open. Pip stood beside the little doorway.

The green pixel hovered.

Then it made a tiny beep.

Beep?

Pip tilted his head. “It says it can go home, but it is worried about the chicken signal staying here.”

Papa inspected the plate. “There may be a few map crumbs left from the pixel bites.”

Bear looked at the little square bite marks.

“We can clean them up,” he said.

Pip’s face brightened. “With napkins?”

“With napkins,” Papa said. “And maybe one official final tiny bite, taken with permission.”

Bear looked at his chicken. “Pip may have one final tiny bite if it helps close the signal.”

Pip put one hand over his heart. “I accept this noble chicken responsibility.”

He took one careful pixel bite from the corner of a tender.

Nibble.

A last green map crumb floated out.

It spun above the plate.

Bear held up the calm map. Papa held the menu sound shade. Pip hummed the soft creeper hum.

The crumb drifted into the little doorway.

The green pixel followed it.

The doorway flashed once.

Not bright. Just cozy.

Then it closed with a tiny sound.

Zip.

No more arrows appeared.

No more pixels blinked.

The chicken stayed chicken.

Pip sat down on the napkin ramp, looking very small and very pleased.

“Fixed,” he said.

Papa smiled. “Fixed.”

Bear looked around the restaurant. Nobody seemed to have noticed the whole tiny adventure, except a toddler at the next table who pointed at Pip and said, “Square frog.”

Pip waved politely.

“I am close enough,” he said.

Bear laughed.

Chapter 5

Papa ordered one small side cup.

Not for sauce.

For Pip.

He lined it with a clean napkin and placed one tiny cucumber piece inside, along with the calm map folded very small.

Pip climbed in and sat down with a happy little wiggle.

“This is an excellent restaurant carrier,” he said.

Bear checked the chicken plate. “Are you still hungry?”

Pip patted his square belly. “A tiny bit. But now I will ask first.”

Bear tore off one crumb of chicken, smaller than a pea, and placed it beside the cucumber.

“For helping,” Bear said.

Pip took a careful bite.

Nibble.

No arrows appeared.

No pixels blinked.

Just dinner.

Papa leaned back in the booth. “That is how we like chicken. Peaceful.”

Bear nodded. “And not full of confused portal crumbs.”

“Also good,” Papa said.

When they finished eating, Bear carried Pip’s little cup in both hands. Papa paid the check and thanked the Redbird staff. Outside, the night air was cool and gentle.

At home, Pip’s quiet corner was waiting: soft towel, dim light, cozy carrier, cucumber slice.

Pip climbed in and tucked the calm map beside him.

“Goodnight, Bear,” he whispered. “Goodnight, clever Papa.”

“Goodnight, Pip,” Bear whispered back.

Papa tucked Bear into bed. Little Polar Bear watched over the neighborhood. Little Red Bear rested warmly on the deck.

Bear’s eyes were heavy.

“Papa?” he murmured.

“Yes, Bear?”

“If a friend is taking bites of your chicken, you should ask why before getting mad.”

Papa smiled. “Especially if the bites are tiny, square, and full of clues.”

Bear giggled once, then settled.

The house was quiet. The map crumbs were home. The chicken mystery was done.

Goodnight, Bear.
