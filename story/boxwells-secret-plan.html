Boxwell's Secret Plan

Bear bikes his friend Boxwell home for a secret hide-and-seek plan with Cheetah's borrowed shrink ray — and learns turtles aren't very good hiders.

Preamble

The morning was bright and a little breezy — good bike weather. Bear checked his backpack twice before they rolled out: helmet snug, water bottle full, and one borrowed silver machine tucked deep inside, humming very quietly.

“Ready?” Papa asked, swinging onto his bike.

“Ready,” Bear said, patting his backpack like it held a secret. It did.

They pedaled down the sunny path toward the creek, gravel crunching, dragonflies zigzagging over the water. Near a sunny flat rock, something small and green blinked up at them.

“Hello,” said a slow, friendly voice. “I'm Boxwell.”

Bear grinned so wide his cheeks hurt.

He had a feeling this turtle was about to become part of the plan.

Chapter 1 — The Plan at the Creek

Boxwell blinked up at them slowly, like everything he did had all the time in the world.

“You bike fast,” he said to Bear.

“You blink slow,” Bear said back, and Boxwell laughed a little wheezy turtle laugh.

Papa crouched by the water, refilling his bottle. Bear leaned close to Boxwell's shell and lowered his voice to the smallest whisper he owned.

“Do you want to play hide and seek? At my house. With something amazing.”

Boxwell's eyes brightened. “I am an extremely good hider. I have a shell.”

“This is better than a shell,” Bear whispered. “I have a shrink ray. My friend Cheetah let me borrow it. If we shrink you down, you could hide anywhere. Under a leaf. Inside a shoe. Nobody would ever find you.”

Boxwell considered this with the seriousness of a turtle voting on something important.

“I accept,” he said.

Bear glanced at Papa, who was busy coiling a bike lock and humming to himself.

“Papa,” Bear called, “can Boxwell and I ride home first? You can bring the bikes slow.”

Papa looked up, unbothered. “Stay on the path. Straight home. I'll be right behind you.”

“We will!” Bear said, already unhooking his bike.

He did not mention the shrink ray. That part was still just his and Boxwell's.

Boxwell climbed into Bear's backpack beside the humming silver machine, tucking in his legs with a small contented sigh.

“Comfortable?” Bear asked, pedaling now, gravel spitting beneath his tires.

“Very,” Boxwell said. “This is faster than I have ever gone in my whole life.”

They flew down the sunny path together, backpack bouncing, turtle giggling softly with every bump.

At Papa Bear Home, Bear skidded to a stop by the deck, breathless and grinning. He unzipped the backpack, and Boxwell peeked out at the porch, the wind chimes, and Little Red Bear and Little Polar Bear resting peacefully in the sun.

“Welcome to my house,” Bear said.

He lifted the shrink ray out carefully, both hands, the way Cheetah had shown him.

“Ready to be the best-hidden turtle in the world?”

Boxwell nodded, slow and sure.

“Ready.”

Chapter 2 — Reading the Rules

Inside, Bear set the shrink ray on the kitchen table and pulled out a folded instruction card taped to its side. He had read it once before, at Cheetah's house, but this time it felt more important.

“Rule one,” Bear read aloud, tracing the words with his finger. “Recommended tiny time: twenty minutes.”

“Twenty minutes,” Boxwell repeated, testing the words like new pebbles. “That is not very long.”

“It's plenty,” Bear said. “Hide and seek games are fast. Rule two—careful return mode. After twenty minutes, the machine needs your current height, the exact time you were tiny, and five seconds of standing completely still on this little square.”

He tapped a small marked square on the base of the shrink ray.

“Five seconds still,” Boxwell said. “I am extremely good at still. I am a turtle.”

Bear grinned and found the kitchen timer shaped like a strawberry. He twisted the dial.

“I'll set it for eighteen minutes,” he said. “Two minutes extra, just in case, so we always stop with time to spare.”

“Very responsible,” Boxwell said solemnly.

Bear nodded, feeling proud of himself. Papa always checked twice before trying something new. This was Bear checking twice, all on his own.

Next came the boundaries. Bear grabbed a pencil and the notebook from his science kit.

“Hiding spots,” he said, writing as he spoke. “Kitchen. Living room. Front hallway. Not outside—too many hawks for someone your size. Not the bathroom—too much water. And not under the couch cushions, because Papa sits down fast and doesn't look first.”

Boxwell nodded at each rule, filing them away.

“What do I do if I get worried?” Boxwell asked, his slow voice a little smaller than before.

Bear paused, remembering something Papa always said. “You say so. Right away. Worried is allowed. We just don't hide it.”

Boxwell seemed to relax at that, his shell settling lower, more comfortable.

Bear checked the window. No sign of Papa yet, just the quiet street and a robin hopping along the fence.

“Okay,” Bear said, lifting the shrink ray and aiming it carefully, the way Cheetah had shown him—steady hands, safe distance, clear path. “Any last words before you're tiny?”

Boxwell considered this for a long, thoughtful moment.

“Try not to step on me,” he said.

Bear laughed so hard he nearly dropped the shrink ray. He steadied it, took a breath, and pressed the glowing button.

A soft green light swept over Boxwell.

There was a tiny fwip sound, like a bubble popping backward.

Where a turtle had been sitting, there now sat a turtle roughly the size of a bottle cap, blinking up with the exact same slow, patient eyes.

“Whoa,” tiny Boxwell said, his voice now a squeak. “Everything got very big.”

Bear pressed start on the strawberry timer.

The countdown had begun.

Chapter 3 — The Worst Hider Ever

“Count to twenty!” tiny Boxwell squeaked, scrambling toward the living room at a pace that could generously be called a determined shuffle.

Bear covered his eyes. “One. Two. Three.”

He heard a soft clack. Then another clack. Then a muffled “ow” that was somehow both tiny and dignified.

“Eighteen. Nineteen. Twenty!”

Bear opened his eyes and turned around.

Boxwell was standing directly in the middle of the living room rug, in plain sight, next to absolutely nothing that could hide him.

“Found you,” Bear said.

“No you didn't,” Boxwell said. “I am hiding.”

“You're standing on the rug.”

“I am blending in with the pattern.”

Bear laughed so hard he had to sit down. “Boxwell, the rug is blue with yellow stars. You are green.”

Boxwell looked down at himself, considering this new information with great seriousness. “That does seem like a design flaw.”

“Try again,” Bear said, closing his eyes and counting once more.

This time, the clacking went on much longer. Bear heard several small thumps, a scrape, and what sounded suspiciously like Boxwell talking to a houseplant. When he opened his eyes, he found Boxwell wedged halfway behind the same skinny lamp as before, shell sticking out on both sides, twice as visible as if he'd hidden nowhere at all.

“I like this lamp,” Boxwell explained. “It has a nice shadow.”

“Half of you is not in the shadow.”

“The important half is.”

Bear tried not to laugh again and completely failed.

They kept going. Boxwell hid behind a single sock. He hid inside an empty tissue box, tipped sideways, four legs waving slowly in the air like a stuck beetle. Bear had to gently roll him upright, both of them giggling so hard that tears came to Bear's eyes.

“I think,” Boxwell said, lying on his back and blinking at the ceiling, “that turtles were not built for this game.”

“You are extremely bad at it,” Bear said, wiping his eyes. “It's the best thing I've ever seen.”

For his next attempt, Boxwell announced he had found the perfect spot and vanished behind the couch leg—except the couch leg was thin, round, and nowhere near wide enough to hide a turtle of any size. Bear found him instantly, shell peeking out on all four sides like a small green moon eclipsing a very small sun.

“This is actually impossible,” Boxwell admitted. “I do not understand shadows the way I thought I did.”

“Maybe hiding isn't about shadows,” Bear said, trying to sound encouraging. “Maybe it's about small spaces.”

Boxwell brightened at that and marched—slowly, proudly—toward a shoebox left open by the closet. He climbed in, pulled the lid mostly shut, and went still.

Bear counted to twenty and searched the whole room twice before he heard it: a tiny, muffled voice from inside the shoebox, humming softly to pass the time.

“You're humming,” Bear said, lifting the lid.

“Hiding is boring,” Boxwell said. “I did not know that part.”

He tried once more, squeezing himself under the couch cushion despite the rule, just the very edge, one back leg still sticking straight out and wiggling in the open air like it had a mind of its own.

“I can see your foot,” Bear said.

“That is not my foot. That is a very foot-shaped shadow.”

Bear collapsed onto the rug laughing, clutching his stomach, and Boxwell—satisfied with his own explanation—settled in to wait, one leg still proudly waving.

“Boxwell,” Bear said between giggles, “you might be the worst hider I have ever met.”

“I prefer,” Boxwell said with great dignity, “to think of myself as extremely findable. It is a different skill.”

Bear laughed until his sides hurt, flopping back onto the rug next to his tiny, terrible, wonderful new friend.

Neither of them looked at the strawberry timer, still quietly counting down on the kitchen counter, red numbers ticking past without a sound.

Chapter 4 — Twenty-One Minutes

Bear was mid-laugh, wiping his eyes, when the front door clicked open and Papa's voice called, “I'm home! Bikes are locked up.”

Bear's stomach did a small flip. He glanced at the strawberry timer on the counter.

It read -3:00, counting up in red numbers.

“Boxwell,” Bear said, his voice suddenly tight. “The timer.”

Boxwell craned his tiny neck toward the counter. “Oh no.”

“You've been tiny for twenty-one minutes,” Bear said, his heart starting to pound. “That's past the twenty-minute time. That's past even my extra time.”

“Is that bad?” Boxwell asked, and for the first time, his slow voice wobbled.

“I don't know!” Bear's hands were shaking now. He grabbed the shrink ray off the table, fumbling with the dial. “I have to do the return thing, height and time and standing still, but I don't remember which number goes where, and what if I do it wrong, what if—”

“Bear.”

Papa's voice was calm, close, already kneeling beside them on the rug. He must have heard the shake in Bear's words from the hallway.

“Papa, I shrank Boxwell, and it's been too long, and I don't know if I'm doing this right—”

“I know,” Papa said gently. “I can see. Let's breathe first. Just for one second. In.”

Bear breathed in, shaky and too fast.

“And out.”

Bear breathed out. It came out shaky too, but a little slower.

“Boxwell, can you breathe with us?” Papa asked.

“I don't have lungs the way you do,” Boxwell said, “but I can try to be still, if that helps.”

“It helps,” Papa said, smiling gently. “Being still is its own kind of breathing.”

They did it twice more—in, out, in, out—until Bear's hands stopped shaking quite so much.

“Okay,” Papa said. “Now. Tell me exactly what the card says. Not from memory. Read it.”

Bear found the folded instruction card, hands steadier now, and read aloud: “Current height. Exact time tiny. Five seconds still on the measuring square.”

“Good,” Papa said. “One at a time. What's Boxwell's current height?”

Bear grabbed his science-kit ruler and measured with careful, gentle hands. “Four centimeters.”

“Type it in,” Papa said. “Now, exact time.”

Bear checked the timer. “Twenty-one minutes and forty seconds.”

“Type it in,” Papa said again, steady as ever. “Now the square.”

Boxwell shuffled onto the marked square on the base of the shrink ray. Papa knelt close, one hand hovering nearby but not touching—ready, not rushing.

“Five seconds,” Papa said. “I'll count. One. Two.”

Boxwell went completely, perfectly still—the stillest he had been all afternoon.

“Three. Four. Five.”

Bear pressed the glowing button.

Green light swept over Boxwell. There was the same small fwip, backward this time, like a bubble growing instead of popping.

Where a tiny turtle had been standing, a turtle-sized turtle now sat on the rug, blinking normal-sized eyes, shell rising and falling with one long, relieved breath.

“That,” Boxwell said, in his regular slow voice again, “was the most exciting twenty-one minutes and forty seconds of my entire life.”

Bear hugged him, careful and tight, laughing with relief instead of nerves this time.

Chapter 5 — Home Size

The three of them sat together on the living room rug, the shrink ray safely switched off between them.

“So,” Papa said, in the easy, curious tone he used when he wanted the whole story, not a lecture. “Hide and seek?”

“Boxwell wanted amazing hiding spots,” Bear said. “He's not very good at hiding, though. Even tiny.”

“I am extremely findable,” Boxwell agreed, with no small amount of pride. “It is my one true talent.”

Papa laughed, the warm kind that meant he wasn't upset, just glad to be let in. “Next time, tell me before the shrinking part starts. Secrets between friends can be good. But new sizes are safer with three sets of eyes instead of two.”

Bear nodded. “I did read the rules first. I set an extra-safe timer.”

“You did,” Papa said. “I'm proud of that part. You just also needed someone watching the clock who wasn't busy laughing at a turtle in a shoebox.”

“That part was very funny,” Boxwell said solemnly.

Outside, the afternoon had softened into golden evening. They carried Boxwell to the deck, where Little Red Bear and Little Polar Bear sat exactly where they always sat, peaceful in the fading light.

“Are they hiding too?” Boxwell asked.

“Just resting,” Bear said. “Better at still than you, probably.”

“Everyone is better than me at still,” Boxwell said. “I am better than everyone at being found.”

Papa knelt to Boxwell's level. “Thank you for trusting us today—the plan, and telling us when it got scary.”

“Bear did the telling,” Boxwell said. “I mostly did the panicking.”

“That's allowed too,” Papa said gently.

Bear yawned, the whole day settling warm in his chest—bikes, a new friend, a countdown, and Papa's steady voice pulling them both back to calm.

“Regular size tomorrow,” Boxwell decided. “I think I've had enough tiny for one week.”

They watched the evening settle over the yard, the creek murmuring past the fence. Bear leaned against Papa's side, already half asleep, one hand resting on his new friend's shell.

The plan had gone sideways. The friendship had not.
