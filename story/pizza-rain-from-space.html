Pizza Rain from Space

Bear and Papa follow pizza rain into the sky and repair a silly satellite stuck in snack mode.

7 parts, played one after another — just press play.

Status: COMPLETE

Preamble

Tonight, Bear and Papa Bear Home was quiet in the cozy way. The telescope waited by the window, pointing up like it had a secret.

Bear pressed one eye to it.

“Papa,” he whispered, “the sky is doing something weird.”

Papa leaned close. “Good weird or uh-oh weird?”

Bear adjusted the little knob. Far above the rooftops, soft pink clouds puffed across the moon. They looked exactly like cotton candy. Then—plop!—a tiny slice of pizza drifted down through the starlight.

Then another.

Then seven.

Papa blinked. “Well. That is not normal weather.”

Bear grinned. “Pizza rain.”

What should Bear and Papa do first?

A) Climb onto the deck with a big umbrella and investigate the pizza rain. B) Use the telescope to follow where the pizza is coming from. C) Build a silly Pizza Weather Detector in Papa’s workshop.

Chapter 1

Bear twisted the telescope knob very slowly.

The pizza slice in the lens went blurry. Then sharp. Then blurry again.

“Easy does it,” Papa said. He knelt beside Bear so their shoulders were almost even. “Tiny turns. Big looking.”

Bear made the tiniest turn he could. The falling pizza came into focus. It was not dropping straight down. It was floating in a long, wiggly line, like a cheesy orange ribbon across the sky.

“It’s coming from over there!” Bear said.

Papa followed Bear’s pointing finger. Above the rooftops, past Little Polar Bear’s lookout spot, the cotton-candy clouds were gathered around one bright twinkly dot.

The dot blinked red.

Then green.

Then red again.

“That looks like a signal,” Papa said.

“From a pizza planet?” Bear asked.

“Possibly,” Papa said. “Or a pizza machine. Or a very confused satellite with dinner plans.”

Plop.

A tiny pizza slice landed on the deck outside. It had one pepperoni and a little triangle of mushroom. Steam curled off it in the cool night air.

Bear looked at Papa.

Papa looked at Bear.

“No eating mystery sky pizza until we understand it,” Papa said.

Bear sighed. “That is probably wise.”

They carried the telescope carefully onto the deck. Little Red Bear sat there, warm and still, watching the pizza rain patter softly on the boards. Far away, Little Polar Bear’s statue seemed to face the bright blinking dot, as if he was keeping watch too.

Bear aimed the telescope higher. The dot became a small silver shape with tiny spinning paddles.

Papa’s eyebrows went up. “A weather satellite.”

Bear gasped. “A pizza weather satellite?”

The satellite gave a cheerful little flash. Then the cotton-candy cloud beside it puffed bigger and rumbled.

Not thunder.

A tummy rumble.

Then twenty pizza slices started drifting down at once.

Papa opened the big deck umbrella with a fwump.

Bear laughed as pizza tapped the umbrella like soft, cheesy rain.

“We need to figure out why that satellite is making dinner weather,” Papa said.

Bear nodded. “And fast. Before the whole neighborhood smells like pizza.”

What should Bear and Papa try next?

A) Count the satellite’s red-and-green flashes to see if it is sending a message. B) Catch one pizza slice in a bowl and inspect it like scientists. C) Ask Little Polar Bear to help them watch the sky while they follow the pizza trail.

Chapter 2

Bear looked at the falling pizza. Then he looked at the blinking satellite.

“We need to go up there,” he said.

Papa nodded slowly, the way he did when an idea was big but not too big. “No cars, I assume?”

“No cars,” Bear said. “Cars are for roads. This is a sky problem.”

“Excellent classification,” Papa said.

They hurried to Papa’s workshop. The roof tap-tapped with pizza rain. The air smelled like warm cheese and cotton candy, which was nice for about twelve seconds and then became very, very a lot.

Papa pulled a tarp off a small silver flying machine with stubby wings, a round window, and two booster rockets strapped underneath.

Bear’s eyes went wide. “Papa.”

Papa smiled. “The Sky Scooter. Technically, it is part plane, part rocket, and part very good seatbelt.”

Bear climbed in and clicked his seatbelt. Papa checked it once. Then twice. Then he checked his own.

“Ready?” Papa asked.

Bear held up his hands. “Ready.”

The Sky Scooter rolled out onto the deck. Little Red Bear sat still beside the door, looking very official. Little Polar Bear watched from his place in the neighborhood, facing the cotton-candy clouds.

Papa pressed the launch button.

Fwoooooosh!

The Sky Scooter lifted gently, not like a cannon, but like a balloon that had remembered it was also a rocket. Bear laughed as the deck grew smaller. The townhomes became blocks. The streets became ribbons. The pizza rain drifted past the window in slow, spinning slices.

One slice stuck to the glass.

“Windshield pizza,” Bear said.

“Not ideal for visibility,” Papa said, and flicked on the wipers.

Squeak. Squeak. Squeak.

The wipers pushed the slice away. It floated off toward a cotton-candy cloud and disappeared with a tiny pop.

Bear leaned toward the telescope screen on the dashboard. “There’s the satellite!”

Up close, it did look broken. One little panel was wobbling. A tiny hatch opened and closed by itself. Red-green-red-green flashed from a light on the side.

Papa pointed to the pattern. “That is not a scary signal. It’s more like a warning light.”

“Like when a toy needs batteries?” Bear asked.

“Exactly like that, except the toy is in space and raining pizza.”

The Sky Scooter eased beside the satellite. A label on its side read: WEATHER SNACK TESTER — PROPERTY OF THE SILLY SKY LAB.

Bear read it carefully. “Snack tester?”

The cotton-candy cloud beside them gave another tummy rumble.

Papa tapped the screen. “I think the satellite is supposed to test weather. But this panel is stuck on snack mode.”

Bear grinned. “So it thinks clouds should make pizza.”

“Which is creative,” Papa said. “But messy.”

A small toolbox floated out from under Bear’s seat, clipped safely to a cord.

Inside were three tools: a star-shaped screwdriver, a soft cleaning brush, and a big friendly button labeled RESET, BUT POLITELY.

Bear looked at the wobbling panel. It rattled harder.

Clank-clank-clank.

Then the satellite sneezed.

“Achoo-pizza!”

Six tiny slices puffed out and bonked gently against the Sky Scooter.

Papa took one slow breath in. Then out. Bear copied him.

“Okay,” Papa said. “We can fix one thing at a time.”

What should Bear and Papa do first?

A) Use the star-shaped screwdriver to tighten the wobbling snack panel. B) Use the soft brush to clean cotton-candy fuzz out of the hatch. C) Press RESET, BUT POLITELY and see if the satellite remembers normal weather.

Chapter 3

Bear pointed to the tools one by one.

“Clean,” he said. “Tighten. Then reset.”

Papa’s face lit up. “That is an excellent repair order.”

“Because if we reset it while it is still fuzzy and wobbly,” Bear said, “it might just get confused again.”

“Exactly,” Papa said. “Clean the problem. Fix the loose part. Then restart the system.”

Bear picked up the soft cleaning brush. It had blue bristles and a handle shaped like a comet. Papa clipped the safety cord from the brush to Bear’s wrist loop.

“Tool secured,” Papa said.

“Scientist secured,” Bear said, patting his seatbelt.

Papa opened the Sky Scooter’s little side arm. It stretched out gently, like a helpful robot elbow, and held Bear close enough to reach the satellite hatch without floating away.

The hatch went flap-flap-flap.

A puff of pink cotton-candy fuzz drifted out.

It stuck to Papa’s nose.

Bear giggled.

Papa crossed his eyes trying to see it. “I appear to have been clouded.”

Bear brushed the hatch carefully. Swish. Swish. Swish.

The fuzz came loose in soft clumps. It floated around the window like tiny pink sheep. The satellite made a grateful little beep.

Not Beep the robot. Just a regular beep.

The hatch stopped flapping.

“Clean!” Bear said.

“One,” Papa said, holding up a finger.

Bear picked up the star-shaped screwdriver next. The wobbling panel rattled again.

Clank-clank-clank.

Below them, the cotton-candy clouds puffed bigger. Pizza slices began falling faster. Not dangerous fast. Silly fast. Like the sky had shaken a pizza box upside down.

“Oh,” Papa said. “Snack mode is getting excited.”

Bear leaned forward. “I can do it.”

“I know,” Papa said softly. “Tiny turns. Big fixing.”

Bear set the screwdriver into the star-shaped screw. He turned it once.

Click.

The panel wobbled less.

He turned it again.

Click.

The panel sat straighter.

He turned it one more time.

Click.

The panel stopped moving.

“Tight!” Bear said.

“Two,” Papa said, holding up two fingers.

The satellite’s warning light changed. Red-green-red-green became yellow-yellow-yellow.

Bear frowned. “Is yellow bad?”

Papa looked at the screen. “Yellow means it is ready for the next step. Like when the microwave says one second left.”

The cotton-candy cloud gave the biggest tummy rumble yet.

Rrrrrrrrumbly-bumbly-boom.

A giant pizza slice slid out of the cloud. It was as big as a picnic blanket. It drifted slowly toward the neighborhood.

Bear’s eyes got round.

Papa took one slow breath. “Still silly. Still safe. But we should finish.”

Bear nodded. “Reset, but politely.”

Papa guided the Sky Scooter a little closer. The friendly button glowed soft blue. Under the words RESET, BUT POLITELY, there was a tiny instruction sticker:

ASK NICELY BEFORE PRESSING.

Bear smiled. “That is polite.”

He cleared his throat.

“Dear satellite,” Bear said, “you are doing a very interesting job. But the neighborhood does not need pizza rain right now. Could you please remember normal weather?”

The satellite listened.

At least, it seemed to.

Its little paddles slowed. The cotton-candy fuzz stopped puffing. The giant pizza slice paused in midair, wobbling like a blanket on a clothesline.

Papa whispered, “Now press.”

Bear pressed the button.

Boop.

The satellite went dark.

Just for one second.

Then all its lights came back in a happy rainbow circle.

A voice from the satellite speaker said, “THANK YOU. SNACK WEATHER TEST COMPLETE. RETURNING TO REGULAR CLOUDS.”

The cotton-candy clouds made a soft whooshing sound. Pink turned to white. Sugar puffs turned to mist. Pizza slices all across the sky stopped falling and floated gently upward, shrinking smaller and smaller until they became tiny sparkles.

The giant pizza slice folded itself into a neat triangle, gave one polite little bow, and popped into a star.

Bear pressed his hands to the window. “We fixed the sky.”

Papa smiled. “You picked the right order.”

The satellite blinked once more. A small door opened, and out floated a silver thank-you token shaped like a tiny cloud.

Bear caught it in the toolbox.

Then the Sky Scooter gave a soft ding.

Papa checked the dashboard.

“Hm,” he said.

Bear looked over. “Hm good or hm tricky?”

“Tiny tricky,” Papa said. “The reset worked, but now the satellite wants us to deliver the thank-you token to Little Polar Bear. It says he helped guide the signal from below.”

Bear looked down through the window. Far beneath them, Little Polar Bear stood in his watchful place, glowing softly in the moonlight.

The Sky Scooter turned toward home.

Then one last cotton-candy cloud puffed in front of them.

It was small. Round. And holding a single tiny dessert pizza.

Papa blinked. “Apparently there is a goodbye snack.”

What should Bear and Papa do on the way home?

A) Carefully fly around the little cloud and deliver the token first. B) Ask the cloud to wrap the dessert pizza for later. C) Use the telescope to guide the Sky Scooter safely back to Little Polar Bear.

Final Chapter

Bear waved politely to the little cotton-candy cloud.

“Excuse me,” he said. “Could you wrap the dessert pizza for later, please? We have to deliver something important first.”

The cloud wiggled happily.

It tucked the tiny dessert pizza into a little box made of moonlight and pink fluff. Then it tied the box with a ribbon of mist.

Papa smiled. “Very good cloud manners.”

The cloud puffed once, proud of itself, and drifted away to become a normal white cloud near the moon.

Papa guided the Sky Scooter down through the quiet sky. No more pizza rain fell. No more cotton-candy clouds rumbled. The stars shone clean and bright, and the townhomes below looked sleepy and safe.

Bear held the silver cloud token in both hands.

When they landed, Little Polar Bear was waiting in his watchful place. He did not need to move to seem glad. The moonlight rested on his back, and the last sparkle from the fixed satellite twinkled above him.

Bear stepped close and placed the token carefully at Little Polar Bear’s paws.

“Thank you for helping guide the signal,” Bear said.

Papa put a hand on Bear’s shoulder. “A good helper can be far away and still make a difference.”

Little Polar Bear seemed to shine just a little warmer.

Back at Papa Bear Home, Little Red Bear waited on the deck. The moonlight dessert box sat beside him, safe and neat.

Papa opened it. Inside was one tiny dessert pizza, just right for sharing: soft crust, strawberry stars, and a little swirl of marshmallow cloud.

Bear took a small bedtime bite.

“Mmm,” he said. “Space tastes like dessert.”

“Only when properly wrapped for later,” Papa said.

Bear giggled.

Then they went inside. Papa washed Bear’s hands. Bear put on cozy pajamas. The telescope stood by the window again, quiet now, looking up at a sky that remembered how to be sky.

Papa tucked Bear in.

“Tonight you noticed the mystery,” Papa said. “You followed it. You fixed it in the right order.”

“Clean, tighten, reset,” Bear murmured.

“That’s right.” Papa kissed the top of his head. “And then home.”

Outside, Little Polar Bear watched over the neighborhood. Little Red Bear rested on the deck. High above, the satellite blinked one tiny thank-you twinkle.

Bear’s eyes grew heavy.

No pizza fell.

No clouds rumbled.

Everything was fixed. Everything was warm. Everything was home.

Goodnight, Bear.
